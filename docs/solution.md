# Solution: MettaLimb

**Waste becomes a resource.** PET plastic that would otherwise be mismanaged is collected, recycled into filament and 3D-printed into affordable, custom-fit prosthetic parts.

## Three stages
| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Collect** | Gather PET waste (83% is mismanaged in Myanmar), with collection and sorting systems run locally |
| **Recycle** | PET recycling plants and local processing hubs produce filament, with quality control suitable for medical use and room to scale production |
| **Print** | 3D-print prosthetics that are affordable and custom-fit, so users can be served sustainably |

## Circular ecosystem
Affordable prosthetics need cooperation across waste, technology, healthcare and the community.

| Partner | Role |
|---|---|
| Waste suppliers | Provide PET waste and support collection and sorting |
| Recycling and manufacturing | Recycle PET into usable filament and keep material quality consistent |
| Healthcare and rehabilitation | Assess patient needs, support fitting and rehabilitation, validate designs |
| Government and NGOs | Support funding, regulation and policy, and enable community outreach and implementation |
| End users (people with limb loss) | Use the prosthetics and give feedback for continuous improvement |

## Why this combination works
- The raw material is plentiful and, in many places, has little other use
- Local production cuts the cost and delay of importing devices
- Printed parts can be customised per user
- Recycling infrastructure already exists in places such as Yangon (74% PET recycling reported in the data used for the CPPI)

## Evidence the approach is feasible
Rappler and Mizzima (2025) report a free 3D-printed prosthetics programme for Myanmar war amputees that served about 150 patients and produced 40 unique devices in 2025. This is external evidence, not a MettaLimb result.
